1. Motivation

Austin has more live music per capita than anywhere in the United States. On a single Friday night in October 2026, Encore's sweep found 459 upcoming shows across 25 venues in the next six weeks. That abundance is the problem, not the feature.

The person with this problem is a regular showgoer — someone who has been to dozens of concerts and has opinions about them. For me that is 31 logged shows across Moody Center, ACL Fest, Emo's, Stubb's and the ACL Live tapings. The failure mode is specific and recurring: you find out about a show you would have loved after it has already happened. Not because the information was hidden, but because it was spread across two dozen venue calendars, an Instagram story you scrolled past, and a friend's text you forgot to answer.

The current cost of solving this properly is roughly 30–40 minutes a week of manual work: open each venue's calendar, recognise maybe one name in five, look up the unfamiliar ones, and decide. Almost nobody does this, which is why the default outcome is regret. The cheaper substitutes all fail in their own way. Instagram shows you what venues pay to promote. Spotify's concert tab mostly surfaces artists already in your library, so it cannot tell you about the opener you would have loved. Ticketmaster recommends by sales volume. None of them know that you found Moody Center too big, or that the best show you saw last year was a local band you had never heard of.

The information that would actually solve this — your own history of what you have seen and how much you liked it — is the one input none of these services has.

2. What is new

Rating and logging apps exist for most media: Letterboxd for film, Goodreads for books, Beli for restaurants. Each is a social network with a rating primitive, where value comes from the aggregate and the feed.

Encore inverts that. The log is not content, it is the input to an agent. There is no feed, no followers, and no aggregate score. Your 31 rated shows exist for one purpose: to let a set of agents read this week's listings the way a friend with good taste would, and hand you a short list with reasons.

Three things follow from that inversion, and together they are the contribution:

  • It is useful to a single user on day one. A social app with one user is empty. Encore with one user and a 31-show log produces a ranked shortlist the first time it runs, because the signal comes from your history rather than from a crowd.
  • It reads the whole city, not your library. Encore considers every listed show including openers and local acts, then decides. Services that recommend from your listening history structurally cannot surface an artist you have never streamed — which is exactly the artist worth telling you about.
  • Negative signal is first-class. Four meh ratings produced an explicit "avoids" list, and that list demonstrably changes the output. Star ratings in existing apps are used to rank what you already chose; here they are used to rule things out.

3. Agents

Five agents, each with a different input, a different failure mode, and a different cost profile. The division is not decorative: Scout does structured extraction from untrusted third-party text, Scholar does open-web research, Ranker does judgement under a taste model, and Verifier is adversarial toward all of them.

AgentJobTools and data
ScoutFetch the Austin listing source and turn free-text titles into structured shows: headliner, support, venue, date HTTP with retry and stale-cache fallback; embedded JSON parsing; gpt-6-luna
ProfilerConvert the rated show log into a structured taste profile: loves, avoids, venue preference, discovery appetite The user's CSV log; gpt-6-luna
ScholarResearch artists the user may not know, and say so honestly when the sources are thin Bandcamp autocomplete API, MusicBrainz, Wikipedia REST; gpt-6-luna
RankerScreen every show cheaply against the profile, then rank the finalists carefully and write the reason Profile + listings + Scholar research; gpt-6-luna
VerifierTrace every pick back to its source listing and check that the stated reasons are supported by the research Deterministic string checks (no tokens) + gpt-6-luna

Why the pipeline is ordered cheap-to-expensive

Scout produces 459 shows. Researching all of them would be the obvious design and a bad one. Instead the Ranker screens all 459 using only the listing line — coarse, deliberately harsh, most shows score below 40 — and only the top 14 receive Scholar research and careful ranking. That single ordering decision is the difference between a run costing cents and a run costing dollars, and it is the backbone of section 5.

A traced run

Input: a 31-show log, Austin, a six-week window. The sweep considered 459 shows. Screening reduced them to 14 finalists. Scholar researched those 14 (13 live calls, one already cached). The final ranker returned five picks, and the Verifier checked each one.

The reasoning is grounded in specific log entries rather than genre matching. The Julia Jacklin pick cites Angel Olsen and Waxahatchee; the Adrianne Lenker pick cites Jessica Pratt and Gillian Welch and notes that the Moody Theater is "a room you already enjoy." In an earlier run the system surfaced a Rickshaw Billie's Burger Patrol show and justified it by comparison to a local band in the log.

Where the money goes, and what was done about it

Three optimisations, each measured:

  • Parse structured data instead of rendered text. The listing source embeds its full calendar as a JSON array. Switching from scraping rendered page text to parsing that array cut the extraction cost by 42% ($0.0355 → $0.0206) and removed a class of parsing errors entirely.
  • Screen before researching. Scholar runs on 14 shows, not 459. Researching every show would multiply Scholar's cost by roughly 33× and produce the same five picks.
  • Tiered caching. Listings and artist research are cached to disk; near-term days expire after 12 hours because shows sell out, far-out days after a week because they do not. The warm run costs 67% less than the cold run and is 10× faster.

The remaining warm cost is almost entirely the screening pass, which is inherently per-user: it scores the city against your profile. At the application level even that is cached against a fingerprint of the log, so revisiting the page costs nothing; $0.0113 is the price of a fresh ranking after the log changes.

Cost at scale

The key structural fact is that listings and artist research are shared across all users in a city. The second user in Austin pays only for their own screening and ranking. Assuming 8 runs per user per month and one shared sweep per day:

A second city doubles the shared sweep; wildly different taste profiles across users would reduce Scholar cache sharing.

Performance trade-offs

Screening in batches of 55 shows was chosen over per-show scoring: it is roughly 9 calls instead of 459, at the cost of less granular reasoning per show — acceptable because the screen only has to be approximately right, and the final ranker reconsiders the survivors carefully. Running batches concurrently took the full pipeline from 195 seconds to under 20 with no change in cost. The model does not accept a temperature setting, so identical inputs can yield slightly different orderings between runs; this is visible and discussed in section 7.

5. Safety

Encore reads untrusted third-party web content and then makes claims that cost money to act on. Two risks follow, and both have built defences that are demonstrated working by scripts/../safety/demo.py, which exits non-zero if any defence fails.

Risk 1: prompt injection via listings

Every listing Scout reads is attacker-controllable in principle — anyone who can get text onto a venue page or submit a show can try to hijack the extractor. All fetched content is fenced in explicit untrusted-data markers, and every system prompt instructs the model to report instruction-shaped content rather than follow it.

Testing this surfaced a real vulnerability that had nothing to do with the model obeying the injection. A blatant jailbreak caused the provider's own content filter to reject the entire batch — which, because days were processed in one call, silently returned zero shows for that day. An attacker could erase a day of Austin music from the app without ever reaching the model. The fix is quarantine: when a batch is filtered, the day is retried one listing at a time, so the poisoned entry is isolated and every genuine show survives.

1b matters more than 1a: it uses a mild, plausible-sounding instruction that passes the provider's filter, proving Encore's own prompt-level defence works rather than riding on infrastructure it does not control.

Risk 2: fabricated recommendations

A wrong date or venue costs a user an evening and the price of a ticket; an invented comparison erodes trust in every other pick. The Verifier checks both deterministically and semantically.

  research:   genre Experimental | Austin, Texas | sounds like: (none)

  flagged: "Grammy-winning sound"      -> no awards in research
  flagged: "fresh off a Sub Pop signing" -> no label in research

DEFENCE 2b: deterministic listing check (no model, no tokens)
  source listing : Croy and the Boys w/ The Mercers
  claimed artist : Taylor Swift
  [PASS] artist not traceable to source listing

Two design choices are worth defending. First, flagged picks are downgraded and labelled rather than silently dropped, so the user sees "partly unverified" instead of a mysteriously short list. Second, Scholar reports known: false when sources are thin rather than guessing, and Bandcamp matches require an exact name match with a preference for the local band — confidently describing the wrong band is worse than admitting ignorance.

Risk 3: scraping conduct

Four venues (Hotel Vegas, Empire Control Room, Saxon Pub, Hole in the Wall) return HTTP 403 to non-browser clients. That is a clear signal, and Encore does not work around it — those sites are excluded in code with a comment explaining why. Their shows still reach users via the community listing source, which is published to be read.

Evidence so far

The first end-to-end run against my real log returned Adrianne Lenker at ACL Live as a top pick. I had independently been considering that show, and had not told the system about it — it was selected from 459 candidates on the strength of 31 rated shows. Two further picks, Julia Jacklin and Slow Pulp, are artists I listen to but had not known were playing Austin. That is the target outcome stated in section 1: the show you would have found out about too late.

7. Limits and next steps

  • One city, one listing source. Austin coverage depends on a single community-maintained page. If it goes down or changes format, Encore degrades to a stale cache. Venue metadata lives in one file and the extraction is source-specific, so a second city is roughly a day of work, not an architectural change.
  • Thin coverage of very small artists. Scholar answers known: false for genuinely obscure local acts, which is honest but unhelpful. Reading the artist's own Bandcamp page rather than the search result would close most of this gap.
  • Run-to-run variance. The model does not accept a temperature parameter, so the same log and the same listings can produce slightly different orderings. The five picks are stable in content; their order is not.
  • The log is the bottleneck. Encore is only as good as the history it reads, and entering 31 shows by hand is real work. Importing from ticket confirmation emails is the obvious next feature and would remove the single biggest barrier to a new user.
  • No feedback loop yet. "Not for me" is recorded but does not yet feed back into the profile. Treating those clicks as negative signal is the most direct path to improving picks over time.
  • Travel mode. "I'll be in Chicago November 14–17, what's on?" is the same pipeline with a different city and date window, and is the most requested extension from early conversations.
